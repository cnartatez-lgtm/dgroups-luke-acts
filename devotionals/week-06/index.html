Grace in an Ordinary Place

Luke 1:26–38

Before You Begin

These commentaries grow out of this week’s passage. They are not required, and they are not the main thing. They are simply here to help, a little extra for your study. Feel free to use them or not, and to choose the point that best fits your group’s conversation. You do not need to cover everything. What matters most is to read the passage, pray, study it together and let it reach your hearts, so that we respond to God in obedience to his Word. Each point also opens other themes you can explore, as your time and energy allow. Our prayer is that God will guide you, give you wisdom and bring fruit from your time in his Word.

Only the Bible is inspired by God. These commentaries are human reflections, always read them under the authority of God’s Word.

Last week, the angel Gabriel appeared to an old priest inside the temple in Jerusalem. Six months later, he is sent again, but this time to a young woman in a small town in Galilee. Luke wants us to put the two scenes side by side. These three reflections follow the passage: a God who is not limited to one place, a favor that comes before any achievement, and an honest question that God was happy to answer.

God Is Not Limited to One Place

Luke 1:26 to 28 · Exodus 3:1 to 5

The first announcement happened in the most holy place in Israel, at the altar of incense. The second happened in “a city of Galilee named Nazareth” (Luke 1:26), probably in an ordinary house. Luke tells us that Mary was a virgin “pledged to be married” to Joseph. He does not tell us her exact age.

This does not mean the temple was unimportant. God had met his people there for centuries, and Luke’s Gospel begins and ends in the temple. But it also does not mean that small places like Nazareth are automatically more spiritual. The point is about God himself. He is not locked inside one building. Theologians call this God’s omnipresence. It simply means that God is present everywhere. As the psalm says, “Where could I flee from your presence? If I ascend up into heaven, you are there” (Psalm 139:7 to 8).

We see this long before Mary. Moses was looking after sheep “to the back of the wilderness” when God spoke to him from a burning bush. God said, “Take off your sandals, for the place you are standing on is holy ground” (Exodus 3:5). In the ancient world, people often took off their shoes as a sign of respect in a holy place. But this was not a temple. It was a dusty piece of desert. What made it holy was not the place, but God’s presence there. God is not limited by our maps, our borders or the lines people draw between important and unimportant places.

This matters for us. Our gatherings for worship are important, and we should not neglect them. But we must not think that God only works in religious buildings or church meetings. He can meet us in a kitchen, an office, a hospital room or a crowded train.

Reflection Question

God met Moses in a desert and Mary in a small town. How can knowing that God is present everywhere help us expect him and honor him in the ordinary places of our week?

Optional Ideas to Explore

- Genesis 28:16 · “I didn’t know it.” Jacob slept on a stone in the open country, far from home. When he woke up, he said, “Surely Yahweh is in this place, and I didn’t know it.” Sometimes God is closer than we realize.

- Acts 7:48 to 49 · Not in houses made by hands. Stephen reminded the leaders in Jerusalem that “the Most High doesn’t dwell in temples made with hands.” He quoted the prophet, “heaven is my throne, and the earth a footstool for my feet.” Luke’s second book carries this truth from the temple to the whole world.

Favor Before Achievement

Luke 1:28 to 30 · 1 Corinthians 13:1 to 3

Gabriel greets Mary with these words: “Rejoice, you highly favored one! The Lord is with you” (Luke 1:28). Then he adds, “you have found favor with God” (Luke 1:30). Before Mary says anything or does anything, she receives. God’s favor comes first. We can honor Mary’s faith and her beautiful obedience, but the center of the story is not her merit. It is God’s grace.

This has been God’s way from the beginning. In Genesis, God first planted a garden, “and there he put the man whom he had formed” (Genesis 2:8). Adam had not produced anything yet, and he already received everything from God’s hand. Only then did God give him work to do.

This is also how Christian service should begin, with gratitude. Paul was Saul of Tarsus, a persecutor, before he became an apostle, and he never forgot the grace that changed him. Some of the women who traveled with Jesus had been set free from evil spirits or healed of sickness, and they “served them from their possessions” (Luke 8:3). Not every believer has a dramatic story. But every believer has received the greatest miracle, eyes opened to see that we need a Savior.

When our gifts, our service and our generosity come from gratitude, there is less room for complaining and much more joy in serving God. Without love, even our best work slowly becomes noise. Paul says, “If I speak with the languages of men and of angels, but don’t have love, I have become sounding brass or a clanging cymbal” (1 Corinthians 13:1).

Reflection Question

Mary’s story begins with God’s grace, not with her achievements. How can remembering what we have received from God turn our service from a duty we complain about into an act of love and gratitude?

Optional Ideas to Explore

- Luke 7:47 · Forgiven much, loving much. Jesus said of the woman who washed his feet with her tears, “her sins, which are many, are forgiven, for she loved much.” The more we understand how much we have received, the more our love grows.

- 1 Timothy 1:12 to 14 · Paul’s thank you. Paul writes, “I thank him who enabled me,” and remembers that he was “a blasphemer, a persecutor, and insolent.” His whole ministry flowed from the grace of Christ that “abounded exceedingly.”

An Honest Question

Luke 1:34 to 38 · Luke 1:18 to 20

Both Zechariah and Mary asked a question. Zechariah said, “How can I be sure of this?” (Luke 1:18). Mary said, “How can this be, seeing I am a virgin?” (Luke 1:34). At first the questions sound alike. But the story itself shows us they were different. Zechariah was told, “you didn’t believe my words” (Luke 1:20). Mary received a full answer, and later Elizabeth said of her, “Blessed is she who believed” (Luke 1:45). Zechariah asked for proof. Mary asked how God would do it.

There is a surprising contrast here. The one who doubted was a priest, an experienced man, standing in the temple. The one who believed was a young woman from a small town, who had no high position in her society. Faith does not depend on our position or our religious experience. It depends on an honest heart before God.

Sarah’s story helps us here. When she heard that she would have a son in her old age, she “laughed within herself” (Genesis 18:12). Her laugh was hidden, but God saw it. And when she was asked about it, she said, “I didn’t laugh,” because she was afraid (Genesis 18:15). Sarah hid her doubt. Mary brought her question into the open. God already knows what is inside us, so we have nothing to gain by pretending.

Notice also what kind of question Mary asked. She did not ask whether God could do it. She asked how. And God’s answer took her beyond the normal order of life: “The Holy Spirit will come on you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you” (Luke 1:35). God usually works through the normal ways he created, but he is never limited by them. “For nothing spoken by God is impossible” (Luke 1:37). Mary’s answer is the heart of true devotion: “Behold, the servant of the Lord,” and “let it be done to me according to your word” (Luke 1:38).

Reflection Question

God welcomed Mary’s honest question and gave her an answer. How can this help us bring our real questions to God, and make room for others to ask theirs, instead of pretending to be sure?

Optional Ideas to Explore

- Mark 9:24 · Faith and doubt in one prayer. A father who wanted Jesus to heal his son cried out, “I believe. Help my unbelief!” Jesus did not send him away. An honest prayer, even a weak one, is a prayer Jesus hears.

- Acts 17:27 · God is not far away. In Athens, Paul told people who were searching that God wants us to “seek the Lord,” and that “he is not far from each one of us.” Honest seeking is not the opposite of faith. It is often where faith begins.
