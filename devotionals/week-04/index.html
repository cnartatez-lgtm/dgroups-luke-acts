A Light to the Nations

Isaiah 49:1–6

Before You Begin

These commentaries grow out of this week’s passage. They are not required, and they are not the main thing. They are simply here to help, a little extra for your study. Feel free to use them or not, and to choose the point that best fits your group’s conversation. You do not need to cover everything. What matters most is to read the passage, pray, study it together and let it reach your hearts, so that we respond to God in obedience to his Word. Each point also opens other themes you can explore, as your time and energy allow. Our prayer is that God will guide you, give you wisdom and bring fruit from your time in his Word.

Only the Bible is inspired by God. These commentaries are human reflections, always read them under the authority of God’s Word.

In the past weeks we have followed the promise of a King: a throne for David, a child who carries the government, a ruler born in little Bethlehem. Now Isaiah shows us the same hope from another side. God’s chosen one is also a Servant. He speaks God’s word, he knows what it is to feel that his work is wasted, and he is sent as “a light to the nations.” These words are so important that Luke quotes them twice, once at the birth of Jesus and once in the book of Acts. These three reflections follow the passage: the mouth of the Servant, work that seems to bear no fruit, and a mission to the nations that begins in exile.

The Mouth of the Servant

Isaiah 49:2 · Isaiah 6:5 to 7

The Servant says, “He has made my mouth like a sharp sword” (Isaiah 49:2). His main tool is not an army. It is his words. The Bible says that God’s word is “sharper than any two edged sword,” able “to discern the thoughts and intentions of the heart” (Hebrews 4:12). A true word can reveal, confront and heal. But this is not permission for violence or cruel words. And it is not about believing that our words have a special power to create our future. It is about the truth that comes from God.

Jesus is this Servant. When he spoke in Nazareth, people “wondered at the gracious words which proceeded out of his mouth” (Luke 4:22). Even the officers sent to arrest him said, “No man ever spoke like this man!” (John 7:46). Jesus never used words carelessly, never lied and never spoke just to win. Our words are often very different. We speak too fast, or too late, or not at all. Jesus explained why: “out of the abundance of the heart, his mouth speaks” (Luke 6:45). Our mouths show what is in our hearts.

Isaiah knew this. When he saw God’s glory, he cried, “I am a man of unclean lips” (Isaiah 6:5). Some have suggested that his unclean lips may also have included a lack of courage. Perhaps he had seen the sins of his people and their rulers without speaking up as clearly as he should. If so, silence can also be a sin of the mouth.

This invites us to look honestly at how we speak. What do we keep silent so that people will keep accepting us? What do we exaggerate so that we sound more important? What ways of speaking have we learned from the very world we criticize? These questions are not meant to make us afraid to speak. God touched Isaiah’s lips and said, “your iniquity is taken away, and your sin forgiven” (Isaiah 6:7). Then he sent him. Forgiveness comes before the mission.

Reflection question

Jesus never used words to hurt or to win, and he never stayed silent out of fear. How can his way of speaking, and his forgiveness for our unclean lips, shape the way we speak the truth to others?

Optional Ideas to Explore

- Isaiah 50:4 · Words that lift up the tired. The Servant says, “The Lord Yahweh has given me the tongue of those who are taught, that I may know how to sustain with words him who is weary.” The same mouth that is like a sword also knows how to strengthen someone who is exhausted.

- Ephesians 4:15, 29 · Truth spoken in love. Paul calls us to speak “truth in love,” and to say “only what is good for building others up.” Truth without love can wound, and love without truth cannot help. In Christ, both go together.

When Our Work Seems Wasted

Isaiah 49:4 · John 4:37 to 38

The Servant says something surprising: “I have labored in vain. I have spent my strength in vain for nothing” (Isaiah 49:4). This is an honest complaint. We do not need to call it unbelief, and we do not need to pretend he was not tired. But notice how the sentence continues: “yet surely the justice due to me is with Yahweh, and my reward with my God.” He brings his tiredness, and the result of his work, to God.

Isaiah himself spoke many words that he never saw fulfilled. He was part of a much bigger picture. We often think of ourselves as the main character of our own story, and in a small way that is true. But the Bible tells a larger story, the redemption of humanity, and the main character is God. Each of us adds our small part, like one grain of sand on a long beach.

This changes how we measure our work. Some results tell us that we need to change our methods. Other results only show that we want to control things that need patience and depend on God’s grace. We should be careful not to judge any work with human eyes as if our view were final.

Jesus told his disciples, “One sows, and another reaps... Others have labored, and you have entered into their labor” (John 4:37 to 38). Paul said, “I planted. Apollos watered. But God gave the increase” (1 Corinthians 3:6). Many of us come from countries where the gospel arrived through people who never saw the churches that are there today. The missionary William Carey worked in India for about seven years before he saw the first Indian believer baptized. His Bible translations kept bearing fruit long after he died. Whole regions have been reached this way, not by one hero in one generation, but by many faithful people across many generations.

Reflection question

God is the main character of the story, and each of us adds our part. How can this free us from measuring our faithfulness only by the results we can see today?

Optional Ideas to Explore

- 1 Kings 19:10, 18 · “I, even I only, am left.” Tired and afraid, Elijah believed he was the last faithful person in Israel. God answered, “Yet I reserved seven thousand in Israel.” When we feel alone in our work, God sees much more than we do.

- 1 Corinthians 15:58 · Not in vain in the Lord. “Be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the Lord’s work, because you know that your labor is not in vain in the Lord.” The Servant felt his work was in vain, but in Christ no faithful work is ever wasted.

A Light to the Nations

Isaiah 49:6 · Luke 2:30 to 32

The passage begins with a call to people very far away: “Listen, islands, to me. Listen, you peoples, from afar” (Isaiah 49:1). Think about when these words were given. God’s people had been judged and taken into exile. Just before this, Isaiah tells them, “Leave Babylon!” (Isaiah 48:20). They were weak and scattered, and their own restoration had not yet come.

And yet, right there, God speaks to the nations. Bringing Israel back is not enough for him: “It is too light a thing that you should be my servant to raise up the tribes of Jacob... I will also give you as a light to the nations, that you may be my salvation to the end of the earth” (Isaiah 49:6). The mission to the nations is not a plan B because Israel failed. It was in God’s heart from the beginning, when he told Abraham, “All the families of the earth will be blessed through you” (Genesis 12:3). In the middle of their weakness, God made his people look beyond their own borders.

Luke loves these words. When old Simeon held the baby Jesus in the temple, he called him “a light for revelation to the nations” (Luke 2:32). Later, Paul and Barnabas quoted this same verse to explain why they were taking the gospel to the Gentiles (Acts 13:47). That is the story of our whole series, from Bethlehem to the nations.

This means God’s salvation is never limited by our present situation. When we go through hard times, it is easy to see only our own battle. Peter walked on the water while he looked at Jesus, but “when he saw that the wind was strong, he was afraid, and beginning to sink” (Matthew 14:30). Looking to Jesus lifts our eyes. And when our eyes are lifted, we begin to see the people around us too. Many in our church live far from home, almost like the exiles. God can use even that distance to bring his light to others.

Reflection question

In the middle of exile, God gave his people a mission to the nations. How can looking to Jesus, and beyond our own struggles to the people around us, change the way we walk through hard times?

Optional Ideas to Explore

- Acts 13:46 to 48 · From Isaiah to the Gentiles. When some rejected the message, Paul and Barnabas said, “behold, we turn to the Gentiles,” and quoted Isaiah 49:6. “As the Gentiles heard this, they were glad and glorified the word of God.” A promise written in exile became a plan for mission.

- 2 Corinthians 1:3 to 4 · Comfort that reaches others. God “comforts us in all our affliction, that we may be able to comfort those who are in any affliction.” Our own hard times can become the place where God prepares us to help others.
