A Prayer Bigger Than One Family

Luke 1:5–25

Before You Begin

These commentaries grow out of this week’s passage. They are not required, and they are not the main thing. They are simply here to help, a little extra for your study. Feel free to use them or not, and to choose the point that best fits your group’s conversation. You do not need to cover everything. What matters most is to read the passage, pray, study it together and let it reach your hearts, so that we respond to God in obedience to his Word. Each point also opens other themes you can explore, as your time and energy allow. Our prayer is that God will guide you, give you wisdom and bring fruit from your time in his Word.

Only the Bible is inspired by God. These commentaries are human reflections, always read them under the authority of God’s Word.

For four weeks we have listened to the promises of the prophets. Now Luke’s story begins, and the promises start to come true. But they do not begin with a king or a crowd. They begin with an old priest, his wife and a prayer they had prayed for many years. These three reflections follow the passage: a faithful couple in a powerful king’s world, a righteous life without the blessing everyone expected, and an answer to prayer that was much bigger than one family.

In the Days of Herod

Luke 1:5 to 10 · Acts 6:7

Luke begins, “There was in the days of Herod, the king of Judea, a certain priest named Zacharias” (Luke 1:5). Herod was famous and powerful. He built great buildings, including a huge renovation of the temple, and he was so afraid of losing his throne that he killed members of his own family. But Luke mentions Herod only to tell us the date. The camera quickly moves to Zechariah, one priest from the small division of Abijah, and to his wife Elizabeth.

This is the vertical contrast. History remembers the king, but God’s story moves forward through a man burning incense while “the whole multitude of the people were praying outside” (Luke 1:10). There were so many priests that a priest might be chosen to burn the incense only once in his whole life. On that ordinary day of service, God broke a long silence.

There is also a horizontal contrast, and it is important. Later in Luke, Jesus will often confront the religious leaders. Those conflicts bring many hidden things into the light, and Jesus corrects the way the Scriptures were being read so that people could see the heart of God. But Luke does not want us to think that every priest or every religious person was corrupt. He starts his Gospel with a priest and his wife who were “both righteous before God” (Luke 1:6). Simeon and Anna, Joseph of Arimathea and others will follow. And in Acts, “a great company of the priests were obedient to the faith” (Acts 6:7).

This guards us from two mistakes. We should not trust a religious system just because it is old or large. But we should not become cynical either, as if everyone inside it must be false. Even inside an imperfect system, God has faithful people, and he is able to work through them.

Reflection question

God was at work through a faithful priest inside a religious system that Jesus would later confront. How can this help us be honest about failures in general, our own and those around us, while still seeing and honoring the faithful people God has placed among us?

Optional Ideas to Explore

Luke 23:50 to 51 · A good man in the council. Joseph of Arimathea was “a member of the council, a good and righteous man,” who “had not consented to their counsel and deed.” Even in the group that condemned Jesus, God had a faithful man who was waiting for his Kingdom.

Matthew 23:2 to 3 · Right words, wrong lives. Jesus told the people to listen to what the teachers taught from Moses, “but don’t do their works,” because “they say, and don’t do.” Jesus could separate true teaching from the poor example of those who taught it.

Righteous, but Without Children

Luke 1:6 to 7, 24 to 25 · John 9:1 to 3

Luke puts two sentences side by side. “They were both righteous before God, walking blamelessly in all the commandments.” And then, “But they had no child” (Luke 1:6 to 7). In that culture, many people would have looked at a childless couple and asked, “What did they do wrong?” Luke closes that door. Their pain was not a sign that God was against them. And “blameless” does not mean they had never sinned. It means they walked with God sincerely and faithfully.

The New Testament often shows people living without what others would call a blessing, without children, without money, without health or without a good position. People around us may read our situation and draw their own conclusions. What we show, whether we want to or not, can shape how others see us. But that is not the same as what God thinks of us. As God told Samuel, “man looks at the outward appearance, but Yahweh looks at the heart” (1 Samuel 16:7). When Elizabeth finally conceived, she said that God had taken away “my reproach among men” (Luke 1:25). The shame came from people. It never came from God.

There is also a quiet tension here. Zechariah and Elizabeth had prayed for a child for years, and now they were old. Yet they kept serving and kept walking with God. They did not wait for their prayer to be answered before they stayed faithful. This is how faith often grows in hard seasons. We keep praying, we keep serving, and slowly we learn gratitude and dependence, even while some prayers seem to have no answer.

This also teaches us how to walk with others. Infertility, grief and disappointed hopes are real in many lives, sometimes in our own groups. The worst thing we can do is look for a hidden sin to explain the pain. The best thing is to stay close, pray with them and remind them how God sees them.

Reflection question

God saw Zechariah and Elizabeth as righteous, even when others saw only what they lacked. How can this truth comfort us, and help us walk with others, in seasons when the blessing we long for has not come?

Optional Ideas to Explore

John 9:1 to 3 · “Who sinned?” When the disciples saw a man born blind, they asked, “who sinned, this man or his parents?” Jesus answered, “This man didn’t sin, nor did his parents, but that the works of God might be revealed in him.” Suffering is not always a punishment, and God can work in it.

Luke 2:36 to 38 · Anna in the temple. Anna had been a widow for many years and “didn’t depart from the temple, worshiping with fastings and petitions night and day.” Her life looked empty to many people, but she was one of the first to recognize the child Jesus.

More Than What Was Asked

Luke 1:13 to 17 · 1 Samuel 1:27 to 28

Gabriel tells Zechariah, “Don’t be afraid, Zacharias, because your request has been heard. Your wife, Elizabeth, will bear you a son” (Luke 1:13). God answered the prayer. But he did not only give a son. He gave the son a calling. “Many will rejoice at his birth,” and “he will turn many of the children of Israel to the Lord their God” (Luke 1:14, 16). The answer to one family’s prayer became good news for a whole nation.

The story of Samuel is similar. Hannah prayed for a son, and God gave her one. Then Samuel became a prophet for all Israel, “from Dan even to Beersheba” (1 Samuel 3:20). In both stories, a private prayer receives an answer that reaches far beyond one home.

We must be careful here. This does not mean that every time we ask God for bread, he will add coffee, cheese and ham. God is not a machine that always gives more of what we want. The point is different. God’s answers are part of his bigger plan, and his plan always includes other people, because his heart is open to all, not only to some. Paul says God “is able to do exceedingly abundantly above all that we ask or think” (Ephesians 3:20). Often that “more” is not more for us, but more through us.

And look at what John’s calling was. He would come “in the spirit and power of Elijah, ‘to turn the hearts of the fathers to the children,’ and the disobedient to the wisdom of the just,” in order “to prepare a people prepared for the Lord” (Luke 1:17). Preparing people for the Lord meant restoring relationships, in families and in the whole community. Turning to God and turning toward one another belong together.

Reflection question

God answered Zechariah’s prayer with a gift that blessed many others. How can this widen the way we pray, so that our requests make room for what God wants to do through us for others?

Optional Ideas to Explore

1 Samuel 1:11, 27 to 28 · A child given back. Hannah asked for a son and promised, “I will give him to Yahweh all the days of his life.” When the answer came, she said, “I prayed for this child, and Yahweh has given me my petition.” Receiving God’s gift and offering it back to him go together.

Luke 3:10 to 14 · What turned hearts look like. When people asked John, “What then must we do?” he spoke about sharing coats and food, being honest at work and not using power to hurt others. Preparing for the Lord is very practical. It changes the way we treat people.
