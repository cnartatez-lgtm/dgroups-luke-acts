When God Answers with a Child

Isaiah 9:1–7

Before You Begin
These commentaries grow out of this week’s passage. They are not required, and they are not the main thing. They are simply here to help, a little extra for your study. Feel free to use them or not, and to choose the point that best fits your group’s conversation. You do not need to cover everything. What matters most is to read the passage, pray, study it together and let it reach your hearts, so that we respond to God in obedience to his Word. Each point also opens other themes you can explore, as your time and energy allow. Our prayer is that God will guide you, give you wisdom and bring fruit from your time in his Word.

Only the Bible is inspired by God. These commentaries are human reflections, always read them under the authority of God’s Word.

Last week, in 2 Samuel 7, God promised David a throne that would last forever. More than two centuries later, that promise seemed very far away. The king on David’s throne was afraid, and the great empire of Assyria was at the door. Into that darkness, Isaiah speaks some of the most hopeful words in the whole Bible. These three reflections follow the passage: where God’s light shines first, why God answers a crisis of power with a child, and what kind of kingdom his zeal will build.

Where the Light Shines First

Isaiah 9:1 to 2 · 2 Kings 15:29

Isaiah 9 begins with names on a map: “the land of Zebulun and the land of Naphtali ... Galilee of the nations” (Isaiah 9:1). This is not decorative poetry. These were real places with real wounds.

Zebulun and Naphtali were in the north of Israel, a border land. When armies came from the north, like the Arameans, the Assyrians and later the Babylonians, this land was the first to be hit. In Isaiah’s time, the Bible tells us what happened: “Tiglath Pileser king of Assyria came and took ... Galilee, all the land of Naphtali,” and “he carried them captive to Assyria” (2 Kings 15:29). Cities were taken, land was lost and families were forced to leave. The Assyrians often moved whole peoples so they would be too weak to rebel. That is why Isaiah says this land was “brought into contempt.” It was humiliated.

So the darkness of verse 2 is not first a sad feeling. It is war, death, lost homes and exile. But Isaiah 8 shows another darkness too. Many looked to the dead for answers instead of God, they cursed “their king and their God,” and everywhere they looked they saw only “distress, darkness, and the gloom of anguish” (Isaiah 8:19 to 22). The darkness was outside and inside. We should not make it only a private feeling, and we should not make it only a political problem. In Isaiah, it is both.

Over the centuries, Galilee became a mixed region, with many people who were not Jewish living in and around it. By the time of Jesus, many in Jerusalem looked down on it. The religious leaders said, “Search and see that no prophet has arisen out of Galilee” (John 7:52). Even Nathanael, a Galilean himself, asked about a nearby village, “Can any good thing come out of Nazareth?” (John 1:46).

And yet this is exactly where God’s light shines first. Not in the capital, but at the wounded edge, among people others had written off.

Reflection question
Many people around us, and sometimes we ourselves, carry darkness from sin, war, exile, poverty or loss, and also deep confusion about God. When we speak of Jesus as the light, how can we take darkness seriously, and how can Jesus shine over that darkness?

Optional Ideas to Explore

Matthew 4:13 to 16 · The light comes to Galilee. Jesus left Nazareth and made his home in Capernaum, “in the region of Zebulun and Naphtali,” so that Isaiah’s words would come true: “the people who sat in darkness saw a great light.” The land that suffered first was the first to receive the light.
Acts 2:7 · Galileans for the nations. On the day of Pentecost, the crowd was amazed and asked, “Aren’t all these who speak Galileans?” The people from the region others looked down on became the first witnesses of Jesus to the nations.
Luke 1:78 to 79 · The dawn from on high. Zechariah, the father of John the Baptist, uses Isaiah’s picture when he praises God for “the dawn from on high” that comes “to shine on those who sit in darkness and the shadow of death,” and “to guide our feet into the way of peace.” Jesus does not only shine on our darkness. He also guides our steps.

When God Answers with a Child

Isaiah 9:4 to 6 · 2 Corinthians 12:9 to 10

Look at the contrast in verses 5 and 6. First, “all the armor of the armed man in the noisy battle, and the garments rolled in blood” are thrown into the fire. Then comes the answer: “For a child is born to us. A son is given to us.”

Assyria was the superpower of that time, famous for its army and its cruelty. And King Ahaz in Jerusalem, a son of David, was afraid. His solution was to find a bigger army, so he sent a message to the king of Assyria: “I am your servant and your son. Come up and save me” (2 Kings 16:7). God’s answer was very different. Against the war machine of an empire, he promises a baby.

This forces us to rethink what strength really is. A baby cannot fight or give orders. Yet “the government will be on his shoulders,” and he will be called “Mighty God.” In verse 4, the oppressor’s rod was on the people’s shoulder. Now the weight moves to the shoulders of the child. As we saw last week, the true King carries his people.

Christians see this hope fulfilled in Jesus. The eternal Son of God became a real child, and the sign for the shepherds was “a baby wrapped in strips of cloth, lying in a feeding trough” (Luke 2:12). But this is still the promise to David. The child is born to sit “on David’s throne” (Isaiah 9:7). The baby is the King.

This does not mean that weakness is good in itself. Children need protection, and the poor need justice. The point is that God takes the initiative, and he often begins his greatest work in what looks too small to matter. J. I. Packer, a well known Bible teacher, wrote a book with a simple title, Weakness Is the Way. Paul learned this too. Christ told him, “My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness,” and Paul could say, “when I am weak, then am I strong” (2 Corinthians 12:9 to 10).

Reflection question
Jesus came into the world as a small and weak child. How can this help us see God’s greatness in small things, in weak people, and in broken lives, and give us hope in our own weakness and struggles?

Optional Ideas to Explore

Judges 7:2 · The day of Midian. Isaiah 9:4 remembers Gideon’s victory. God reduced his army to three hundred men, “lest Israel brag against me, saying, ‘My own hand has saved me.’” As we saw last week, God often works in a way that leaves no room for our pride.
Zechariah 4:6, 10 · The day of small things. After the exile, God’s people were few and weak, and rebuilding the temple looked impossible. God said, “Not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit,” and asked, “Who despises the day of small things?” It opens the theme of humble beginnings, in our own lives and in the church.

A Kingdom of Justice That God Will Finish

Isaiah 9:5 to 7 · Matthew 26:52

Verse 7 brings us back to last week’s promise: “Of the increase of his government and of peace there shall be no end, on David’s throne.” Even when David’s sons were failing, God had not forgotten what he swore to David.

This verse tells us two things about the kingdom. It is built “with justice and with righteousness.” And in the end, it does not depend on us: “The zeal of Yahweh of Armies will perform this.” Zeal is a burning, passionate love. God is not watching from far away. He is deeply committed to finishing what he promised.

But if God will do it, do our choices still matter? Yes, very much. God’s guarantee does not make right living unimportant. It shows us what kind of kingdom we are waiting for. If we hope for a kingdom of justice, we begin to live by its justice now. Our hope for the future shapes our faithfulness today.

This kingdom also comes in a different way. In history, many people have used violence to build what they believed would be a perfectly just world. Isaiah says the armor and the bloody garments will be burned (verse 5). God shows his power through love and wisdom, through weak means, and above all through the cross. When Peter took a sword to defend him, Jesus said, “Put your sword back into its place” (Matthew 26:52).

The film A Hidden Life, by Terrence Malick, tells the true story of Franz Jägerstätter, an Austrian farmer who refused to swear loyalty to Hitler. He was executed in 1943. People around him asked what his refusal could change. The war would go on, and Hitler would never even notice. Still, he did what was right without seeing any result, and he left the outcome in God’s hands. The title of the film comes from an old novel that speaks of people who “lived faithfully a hidden life.” God’s kingdom often grows like that, through faithful people history does not notice, while the zeal of Yahweh does what we cannot do.

Reflection question
We often see injustice we cannot fix, at work, in our neighborhood or in the news. How can the promise that God himself will finish his kingdom give us courage to keep doing what is right, even when we cannot see the results?

Optional Ideas to Explore

Hebrews 11:13 · Faithful without seeing the end. “These all died in faith, not having received the promises, but having seen them and embraced them from afar.” Many of God’s people were faithful without seeing the result, and God did not forget a single one of them.
1 Peter 2:23 · Leaving judgment to God. “When he suffered, he didn’t threaten, but committed himself to him who judges righteously.” Jesus did not take justice into his own hands. He trusted his Father, and this opens the theme of how we respond when we are treated unfairly.
